# Dub Production & Mixing: A Hands-On Mastery Guide (King Tubby → Black Ark → On-U → Basic Channel)

In dub, the mixing console is the instrument. To master it, learn one fixed set of moves until they are reflexes: a send-and-return feedback loop around a tape echo, a stepped high-pass filter, a spring tank you can strike, mute-based arrangement, and a mono-centred bass and drum foundation. Gear authenticity matters much less. Sources from the period agree that Tubby, Perry, Errol Thompson and Scientist worked on cheap or cast-off four-track systems. What made their sound was routing and performance, not equipment.

## TL;DR
- **What matters most:** Build a dub routing template with aux sends into a tape-style delay and a spring reverb. Bring the returns back on full channel strips that have their own aux sends, so they can feed back into themselves. Add a stepped 18 dB/oct HPF and give every element a mute. Then practise live, one-take passes over a multitrack riddim until you play the moves by feel. *Sound On Sound*'s dub workshop and Mad Professor both describe feedback-loop routing as the basic technique.
- **Gear claims are messy, so check them.** Tubby's "Big Knob" was a passive Altec 9069B high-pass filter on a 1960s MCI board he bought from Dynamic Sounds in 1972. Sources disagree on whether it had 10 or 11 steps (probably 10 frequencies plus an off position). Scientist says the "phasing" people hear on Tubby's records was really the filter. Perry's Black Ark ran a TEAC A-3340 four-track, a Soundcraft board that replaced an Alice mixer around 1975, a Roland RE-201, a Mu-Tron Bi-Phase and a Grampian spring. The often-repeated "Grantham" spring is most likely a garbling of Grampian.
- **Riddims and groovebox design.** Reggae's rhythm conventions (one drop, rockers, steppers, flyers, skank on 2 and 4, organ bubble) are a small, well-defined set. Most of the instruments can be built with simple 2–4-operator FM: a sine-dominant bass with a fast index decay, an inharmonic rimshot, an organ built from drawbar-like ratio stacks, and Chowning-style brass. Build the engine around per-voice mutes, send-level "throws" and a feedback-capable delay. Treat these as core features, not extras.

## Key Findings

1. **"Version" came before "dub."** The most common origin story places it at Duke Reid's Treasure Isle around 1967–68, when Rudolph "Ruddy" Redwood of the Supreme Ruler of Sound system had a dubplate of The Paragons' "On the Beach" cut without its vocal and then played it at a dance in Spanish Town. Either engineer Byron "Smithy" Smith left the vocal off by accident, or, in Bunny Lee's telling (quoted on Wikipedia's "Dub music" page), Redwood said "no, mek it run" and "use to handle that part himself, drop in the voice and drop it out." Other accounts credit Tubby (on "You Don't Care"), and DancehallMag (3 Nov 2026) cites Record Collector's Ian McCann arguing that Prince Buster's echo-drenched "Dark End Of The Street" beat Redwood "good and proper." Treat the "first" claim as contested. The useful lesson is that the vocal-less B-side with selective vocal drop-ins was the seed.
2. **Mad Professor's model of how dub developed:** instrumental "part two" B-sides, then drum-and-bass versions, then reverb returning around 1971–72 ("suddenly you had reverb on the drums"), then echo within months. In his account to *Sound On Sound* (Andrea Terrano, Aug 2007): "Before dub, we used to call it 'bass and drum'. Sound systems and dancers wanted to hear the bass and drum version."
3. **Scientist frames dub as composition by the engineer.** In Tape Op #136 he calls it "a composition from the engineer." He describes the technique as getting "the listener hooked on one pattern, and also how to switch up the pattern," so that "every time you listen to a dub track, it never sounds exactly the same." He told CounterPunch (Stephen Cooper, 13 Feb 2019) that "dub is just the performance." In his words, "the engineering technique… comes first, like the proper tonation and balancing of the instruments."
4. **The studios were small and the sessions fast.** Scientist describes four-hour sessions in which every part was recorded and then mixed straight away, usually in one take.\[1\] In his 2012 LargeUp interview he says "King Tubby's was primarily a mixing and voicing studio because we couldn't track a session there." Rhythms were cut elsewhere, often at Channel One, and the tapes brought to Tubby's.
5. **Black Ark is the exception.** Perry tracked, bounced and mixed his own productions in one room, usually with versions planned from the start.\[2\] That is why Black Ark mixes sound dense and layered rather than stripped back.

## Part B — Studios, Gear & Signal Flow Reference

### B1. King Tubby's, 18 Dromilly Avenue, Waterhouse
- **Console.** A custom 1960s MCI board built by Jeep Harned's company for Byron Lee's Dynamic Sounds.\[5\] Tubby bought it secondhand in 1972 with Bunny Lee's help.\[37\] Descriptions vary: "12-into-4," "16-channel/4-bus," or simply "four-track." MoPOP, which now owns it, calls it a four-track MCI mixing console.\[37\] Scientist says Tubby had no proper mutes and only basic EQ ("add a little bass, take off bass… you can't just go and pick an individual instrument and change it").\[38\] RBMA's Kirk Degiorgio describes a reverb send on every channel and 3-band EQ.\[39\]
- **The "Big Knob" HPF.** An Altec 9069B, a passive inductor-based T-network high-pass filter, 18 dB/oct (3rd order), designed for 600 Ω lines.\[40\] Replica and emulation makers (Audio Merge, AudioThing) list steps at 70, 100, 150, 250, 500 Hz, 1, 2, 3, 5 and 7.5 kHz.\[41\] **Conflict:** some sources say 10 steps, others 11.\[42\] My reconciliation is 10 cutoffs plus off, but this is not confirmed from an Altec datasheet. MoPOP and Wikipedia call it a "parametric EQ," which is technically wrong.\[37\] Rootfire reports it could be patched anywhere through the patchbay and was already in creative use at Dynamic before Tubby owned it.\[43\]
- **Echo.** Steve Barrow's Blood & Fire liner notes say Tubby built his own echo by running a tape loop over the heads of an old two-track machine.\[44\] Forum claims that Tubby used an Echoplex or a Space Echo are **unverified**. The "photo of Tubby with an Echoplex on a Soundcraft" almost certainly mixes him up with Perry's Black Ark.\[45\]
- **Reverb.** King Jammy (in Barrow's interview) says it was a Fisher spring reverb that they modified ("King Tubby and Fisher"), generally taken to be a Fisher K-10 SpaceXpander valve spring unit.\[46\] **Conflict:** Scientist says there "was a Fisher reverb but I didn't use it because it was very noisy."\[47\] Barrow says Tubby physically hit the spring unit to make a "thunderclap."\[44\]
- **Signature sound.** Scientist names two things: "the High Pass filter, and his Reverb Unit."\[38\] He also stresses that "a lot of people believed it was phasers when it wasn't phasers. We just set up the high-pass filter."\[1\]

### B2. Black Ark (Lee Perry), Washington Gardens, 1973–79
- **Tape.** TEAC A-3340 ¼-inch four-track; a TEAC two-track for mixdowns. Perry was later given a TEAC ½-inch 8-track (1979) but rarely used it.\[48\] Engineer accounts say the equipment "barely changed" across the studio's whole life.\[49\]
- **Desk.** An Alice mixer (Perry called it a "toy") until about 1975, then a Soundcraft.\[48\] **Conflict:** the Soundcraft is variously called 16-channel, four-track, Series One, Series 3, or even a Tangent.\[50\] Wikipedia, citing Bradley, says King Tubby designed the studio's circuitry.\[2\] The story that Chris Blackwell sent a 16-track and a console which Perry dumped or buried is folklore.\[49\] Treat it as unverified.
- **Effects.** Roland RE-201 Space Echo, an Echoplex, Mu-Tron Bi-Phase (reportedly an early unit), and spring reverb.\[49\] SOS identifies the spring as a **Grampian**\[51\] (Type 636 per photo-based gear lists).\[52\] "Grantham" in some secondary accounts is most likely a corruption of the name (my inference). A Fisher spring is also mentioned.
- **Drum-bus trick (from Daniel Boyle, who rebuilt the Black Ark rig for Perry's *Back on the Controls*, SOS 2015).** Feed the drum bus into the Grampian, roll off the low end of the return, and blend it back in parallel. This produces the metallic early-to-mid-70s Ark drum sound. Perry also overdrove the Grampian's input with the reverb off, using it as a fuzz on chords. Kick mic: AKG D12 (reportedly also used for vocals).\[51\]
- **Boyle's reconstructed Bi-Phase routing.** Guitar and piano subgroups go to the Bi-Phase.\[53\]\[54\] Its output returns on its own desk channel, which is sent to reverbs and delays, "and then back into itself for feedback loops."\[51\]
- **Bounce-downs.** Perry built density by repeatedly combining tracks to free up space.\[55\] "Twenty tracks" from four is his own poetic boast ("twenty from the extraterrestrial squad"),\[56\] not an engineering count.

### B3. Channel One, Randy's, Joe Gibbs, Harry J
- **Channel One (Maxfield Ave., 1972).** Ernest Hoo Kim reportedly spent whole days with Sly Dunbar perfecting the drum sound. The double tap on the rim on *Right Time* was so new that Sly says people thought it was a sound effect.\[19\]\[20\] The facility was considered high quality, unlike Tubby's or the Ark.
- **Randy's Studio 17.** ET's early dub experiments happened here. Augustus Pablo's KTMRU was recorded at Randy's (plus Dynamic per the sleeve) and mixed at Tubby's.\[57\]
- **Joe Gibbs (Retirement Crescent).** A 16-track room from 1975, where ET mixed the *African Dub* series.\[7\]
- **Harry J.** Rhythms for *Pick a Dub* were tracked here. Sylvan Morris engineered.\[12\]

### B4. UK: Ariwa and On-U
- **Mad Professor's early rig.** He built a desk from a kit and later a customised Soundcraft-based 8-input desk. Before that he had repaired Soundcraft Series 2 EQ boards, telling SOS (Aug 2007): "I probably fixed a hundred of them, so I got the job." Four-track: he "bought myself a four-track Tascam 3440" (SOS 2007). Spring reverb built from a *Practical Electronics* design. Echo made from an Akai tape deck, with the replay-head output returned through an aux to the channel, giving fixed delay times at 3¾ or 7½ ips. That is the Tubby tape-loop approach rebuilt in South London.
- **Ariwa today (SOS 2007).** SSL desk; Otari MTR90 24-track; rack includes Urei LA4 and 1176, dbx 160-series, Drawmer DS201 gates, MXR auto-phasers "hooked up to the desk," Roland SDE-3000/SDE-330 delays, Eventide H3000, Lexicon 480L/PCM80, Alesis Quadraverb, Furman RV1 spring, Pultec, Tube-Tech.\[58\] Ariwa is "permanently wired" for dub. *No Protection* was mixed from two 24-track machines locked together at 30 ips (48 tracks).\[58\]
- **Adrian Sherwood.** Records digitally (Pro Tools) but always mixes "with my fingers using an analog desk."\[25\] He doesn't label the desk, so that he learns where the tune sits across the channels and can play it like an instrument.\[59\] His methods include "plugging a reverb into a phaser, mixing things backwards, turning the mix over,"\[25\] and building tension by going dry, opening up a warped effect, then returning to dry.\[26\] For live shows he uses a large Midas desk.\[60\]

### B5. Canonical routing to recreate (DAW and hardware)

```
[Multitrack stems] → channel strips (each: HPF/LPF, EQ, MUTE, Aux1 Delay, Aux2 Spring, Aux3 Phaser/FX)
Aux1 → Tape delay (RE-201-style) → RETURN CH "DLY" (full strip: EQ/HPF, mute, Aux1 *self-send*, Aux2 → spring)
Aux2 → Spring reverb (+ optional "kick" trigger) → RETURN CH "SPR" (full strip, Aux1 → delay)
Aux3 → Phaser (Bi-Phase-style, 2× 6-stage) → RETURN CH "PHS" (Aux1/Aux2 sends onward)
Master bus: global stepped HPF (Big Knob) insertable pre-master or on a patch point; bass + kick kept centre/mono
Safety on every return: LPF/HPF inside the loop + brickwall limiter (digital) or soft clip (analogue)
```
- **Feedback loop (SOS / Terrano).** Delay of about 0.7 s, set to about four or five repeats. Bring the return up on a *full channel* (not a dedicated FX return without auxes). Raise that channel's own send to the delay, then mute the source once the loop is running, and keep "riding the throttle like a cold car engine." Terrano suggests setting delay time to fall in triplets so the echo "revolves around the beat."\[61\]
- **Digital pitfalls (SOS).** Feedback inside a digital loop can produce harsh comb or "phasy" artefacts and runaway levels. Filter out highs inside the loop and put a limiter on the return.\[61\] Lay out the DAW mixer so every return and mute is visible on one screen or mapped to a controller.
- **Hardware minimum.** A small analogue desk with at least 4 auxes and mutes (Boyle chose his Toft partly because it had six auxes, all in use),\[51\] one tape or tape-model delay, one spring tank you can physically strike, one dual phaser, one stepped HPF.

## Part C — Technique Catalog with Drills

*Note on timestamps:* I could not verify exact timestamps against reference audio, and they differ between pressings and reissues. Below I name the track and where in it to listen. Find your own timestamps with a spectrogram and write them in your notebook. That is part of the drill.

### C1. Mute/drop-out arrangement and mixing as live performance
- **Mechanism.** Arrangement happens at mixdown: channel mutes and fader cuts remove and reintroduce parts against a looping riddim. Tubby mixed live; tracks were "dropped out and brought back as the tape played."\[62\] Musicologist Steve Barrow compares the engineer to a jazz soloist.\[44\]
- **Originators.** Redwood/Smithy (vocal drop-in), Tubby, ET, Scientist.
- **References.** Tubby, "King Tubby Meets Rockers Uptown" (drum-and-bass sections against melodica entries); Scientist, *Heavyweight Dub Champion* (pattern-switching); ET, *African Dub Ch. 3*.
- **Recreation.** Mutes mapped to a pad grid or fader controller. Terrano's method: loop 8 bars and build an arrangement purely with mutes, switching "a fraction before the beat."\[61\]
- **Drill.** With a 4-stem riddim (drums, bass, chop, organ), do ten 3-minute one-take passes with no automation edits. Each pass needs at least one 8-bar drum-and-bass-only section, one bass-out "drop," and one full-band return on the downbeat. Score yourself on timing of the mute relative to the downbeat; the target is landing just ahead of the beat.

### C2. Versioning / riddim reuse
- **Mechanism.** One rhythm track becomes a vocal cut, DJ cut, instrumental and dub. Many producers re-cut classic Studio One and Treasure Isle rhythms (the *African Dub* sets are largely updated versions of those).\[63\] Terrano's Lion Music anecdote: a whole dub album mixed overnight from existing tapes.\[61\]
- **Originators.** Bunny Lee, Coxsone Dodd's rhythm library, Joe Gibbs.
- **References.** Compare Jacob Miller "Baby I Love You So" with "King Tubby Meets Rockers Uptown." Compare Big Youth "S.90 Skank" with Hudson's "Pick a Dub."\[13\]\[64\]\[65\]
- **Drill.** From one riddim, produce four deliverables in one session: vocal mix, instrumental, DJ version (vocal fragments only), full dub. Time-box each to 30 minutes.

### C3. HPF/LPF sweeps (the Big Knob)
- **Mechanism.** A stepped, passive 18 dB/oct HPF switched across 70 Hz to 7.5 kHz. The detented steps give a rhythmic, "clicky" quality rather than a smooth sweep.\[66\] On full mixes or the reverb return, it thins a horn "into a faint squeal, then gone." Scientist: "sometimes you can hear the frequency changin'."\[67\]
- **Originators.** Dynamic engineers (first, more conventional use); Tubby (the musical use).\[43\]
- **References.** "King Tubby Meets Rockers Uptown"; "Dub Magnificent" (Cornel Campbell rhythm); "King Tubby's Rock" (Yabby You).\[68\]
- **Recreation.** AudioThing Dub Filter (11-step emulation; its "impedance" control acts like resonance),\[42\] Audio Merge KTBK-1B hardware, or any HPF with a stepped macro: quantise cutoff to the 10 values above, use 3rd-order Butterworth, and allow zipper switching (no smoothing).
- **Drill.** Put the stepped HPF on (a) the full mix, (b) the spring return only, (c) the drum bus. Perform step changes only on beats 2/4, then only on the "and" of 4. Then do a full "evaporation": 70 Hz to 7.5 kHz in 8 bars, then snap back to bypass on the downbeat.

### C4. Tape-delay feedback, self-oscillation, delay-time throws
- **Mechanism.** Repeats fed back through tape saturation and head-bump EQ. When regeneration goes above unity, the loop rings at its own spectral peaks. Changing tape speed (the RE-201 Repeat Rate) while it is ringing produces pitch glides. RE-201: one record head plus three playback heads, 12 modes (4 echo, 7 echo+reverb, 1 reverb), a 3-spring reverb, Intensity control for feedback and self-oscillation.\[69\] Production started around 1973–74 (sources differ).\[70\]
- **Originators.** Tubby (tape loop on a 2-track), Perry (RE-201/Echoplex), Scientist ("echo explosions"), Mad Professor (Akai replay-head loop).
- **References.** *Scientist Meets the Space Invaders* (feedback blasts); Perry *Super Ape*; Tubby *The Roots of Dub*.
- **Settings.** SOS: about 700 ms, 4–5 repeats, triplet feel.\[61\] Dub Scrolls (community): about 380 ms for chord echoes.\[71\] Roland: dotted 1/16 is "a standard dub echo note time."\[72\] At 75 BPM a quarter note = 800 ms, a dotted eighth = 600 ms, a dotted sixteenth = 300 ms.
- **Recreation.** Roland RE-201 plugin (Roland Cloud or UAD), Boss RE-20/RE-202, Strymon Volante, Echoplex models. In DAWs, a delay with saturation and an HPF/LPF *inside* the feedback path.
- **Drill.** (1) "Single-word throw": open the vocal send for one word only and let it decay. (2) "Engine idle": hold a self-oscillating loop for 16 bars without blowing up or dying, using only the return's self-send. (3) "Pitch dive": sweep Repeat Rate during oscillation so the pitch lands on the riddim's root at the downbeat.

### C5. Spring reverb crashes ("kicking the tank")
- **Mechanism.** Mechanical shock excites all the springs at once, producing a broadband "thunderclap" with a metallic chirp. Tubby physically hit the unit, and Nick Manasseh confirms striking the spring with a stick.\[39\] Short sends on a single snare hit give Terrano's "room shot."\[61\]
- **References.** Tubby *Dub from the Roots*; Scientist *Scientific Dub* (whose opening "pop" through the Tubby spring is cited by collectors).\[46\]
- **Recreation.** A real tank (Fisher K-10, Grampian 636, Accutronics in a DI box) with a piezo pickup; plugins with a "drip" or "crash" trigger. Damping the springs with cloth is a common modification for a darker tone.\[73\]
- **Drill.** Ride a snare send to fire 1–3 room shots per 8 bars at irregular positions. Each shot must clear before the next snare so the result does not smear.

### C6. Phasing/flanging
- **Mechanism.** All-pass cascades (Bi-Phase: two independent 6-stage phasers,\[74\] sweep 0.1–18 Hz, sine or square LFO, Sync Norm/Rev; Phasor B can take Phasor A's output for 12 stages in cascade) on hi-hats, chops and full mixes.\[53\] Note Scientist's caution that much "Tubby phasing" is actually HPF work. Phasing is more of a Perry, Ark and On-U signature.
- **References.** Congos *Heart of the Congos*; Junior Murvin "Police & Thieves"; Max Romeo *War Ina Babylon* (hi-hats and chops).\[50\]
- **Recreation.** Mu-Tron Bi-Phase II, Audio Damage-style Bi-Phase models, Sherwood's "reverb into a phaser." Terrano also suggests a phaser on the delay return, and a gate on a reverb return set to "skatter."\[61\]
- **Drill.** A/B two mixes of the same riddim: (a) Bi-Phase cascaded, slow sine (0.1–0.3 Hz) on the full mix; (b) phaser only on hat and chop, with Rev sync for stereo motion. Then reproduce one Tubby "fake phase" using only stepped HPF moves.

### C7. Varispeed and tape manipulation
- **Mechanism.** Tape-speed changes, bouncing (Perry), flipping the reel to mix backwards (Sherwood: tracks that came back "slightly out of sync" were turned over and mixed in reverse),\[26\] and Boyle's saturating "dry" passes through tape echoes and Revox decks.\[51\]
- **Drill.** Reverse the multitrack, print a 2-minute dub pass, then reverse the print. Separately, slam the bass stem through a tape model at +6–10 dB input and blend.

### C8. Panning and the stereo approach
- **Mechanism.** Many 1970s Jamaican dubs were mono or near-mono, especially those for sound system and 7-inch. Mad Professor says "I took dub into the world of stereo" and praises the early stereo approach of placing whole instruments in separate channels.\[24\]\[58\] Rhythm & Sound and Basic Channel use stereo mainly through effect returns.
- **Practice.** Keep kick, bass, snare and the main vocal centred. Put movement in the returns: Bi-Phase in Rev sync, delay ping-pong only on the returns.
- **Drill.** Mix the same dub in mono first, with no pan moves allowed. Then convert to stereo by panning *returns only*. Check mono compatibility.

### C9. Found sounds and noise
- **Mechanism.** Perry's palm tree with buried microphones as a kick, ritual tape treatments,\[51\] and Conn Rhythm Box.\[75\] ET's telephones and water.\[76\] Tubby's test tones on deep echo.\[44\] Shaka's siren.\[27\] Sherwood's cut-up speech.
- **References.** *African Dub Ch. 3* ("Jubilation Dub" phone, "Freedom Call" water);\[76\] Perry *Revolution Dub*; Jah Shaka *Commandments of Dub*.
- **Drill.** Record ten household sounds and pre-filter them through the spring. Allow yourself at most three appearances per track, each on a structural boundary.

### C10. Vocal snippets and echo throws
- **Mechanism.** Keep enough of the vocal for the crowd to recognise the song, as Tubby did when dropping out "most of the vocal track."\[77\] Throw single words into the delay.
- **References.** *African Dub Ch. 3* (snippets "floating" through);\[76\] Hudson *Pick a Dub* (Big Youth and Horace Andy fragments);\[78\] Tubby/Yabby You "King Tubby's Rock" (studio talk left in).\[68\]
- **Drill.** From a full vocal take, choose no more than 4 phrases per minute, each thrown to delay on its last syllable.

### C11. Bass and drum foregrounding; low end for sound systems
- **Mechanism.** The earliest name for a dub was "bass and drum."\[58\] Bass "sits high in the mix" (Terrano).\[61\] Tubby's own sound system used separate amplification for bass and treble.\[62\] In his 2012 LargeUp interview, Scientist recalls testing amplifiers: "I start to play a song from King Tubby's at the same volume as the rock 'n' roll, all of sudden the transistor started to get hot." Shaka ran valve amps (a "1,000-watt" Metro design) into scoop bins.\[27\]\[30\]
- **Practice.** Mono below about 120 Hz. Keep the bass fundamental clean (sine-like) and put "bite" in 700 Hz–1.5 kHz so it reads on small speakers. Make sure kick and bass don't fight: either the kick sits above the bass fundamental, or the kick is short.
- **Drill.** Mix on a phone speaker and on a subwoofer alternately. The bass line must stay intelligible on the phone, and the sub must not bloom on sustained notes.

### C12. EQ approach
- **Mechanism.** Period consoles had blunt EQ. Tubby had "a little bass, take off bass… highs… mids,"\[38\] so tonal shaping came from the stepped HPF and from Scientist's "proper tonation and balancing" at tracking.\[79\] Treat EQ as a performance control, not corrective surgery. The Ableton forum lore of riding channel EQ on the echo-return channel to change the colour of the feedback matches SOS's in-loop filtering.\[5\]
- **Drill.** Restrict yourself to 3-band shelving or peaking plus one stepped HPF per channel. Mix a full dub with no other EQ.

### C13. Dynamics/compression
- **Evidence gap.** There is little primary documentation of compression practice in 1970s Kingston rooms. Treat any specific "Tubby compressor" claim with suspicion. Documented later practice: Ariwa's LA4, 1176 and dbx units and DS201 gates;\[58\] Boyle's tape compression ("slam it really hard") on the bass;\[51\] SOS's limiter on returns as a safety device.
- **Practice.** Gentle bus compression; hard limiting *only* on feedback returns; gates on reverb returns as an effect.
- **Drill.** Gate the spring return keyed from the snare, and tune hold and release so each crash is chopped into a rhythmic tail.

## Part D — Arrangement & Instrument Conventions (with FM Synthesis Notes)

### D1. Drum patterns
| Style | Grid (16th-note steps, 1 bar of 4/4, "1" = step 1) | Era / source |
|---|---|---|
| **One drop** | Kick + cross-stick/snare on step 9 (beat 3) only; hats in 8ths or swung 8ths | Classic roots; Terrano labels his "Rocker" example as "one drop" |
| **Rockers** | Kick on 1 and 3 (often with pickups), snare/rim on 3; busier, militant feel; Sly's double rimshot | Channel One, introduced around 1976, which pushed aside flyers\[80\] |
| **Steppers** | Kick on every quarter (1, 5, 9, 13), snare/rim on 9 | Channel One onward; the UK sound system standard (Shaka) |
| **Flyers / flying cymbal** | Open-to-closed hi-hat "tssk" on the off-beat 8ths (open on the "and," choked on the next beat) | Santa Davis, 1974, "None Shall Escape the Judgement," adapted from MFSB's "TSOP" (Philly) feel; named by Bunny Lee\[6\] |

- **Tempos.** Roots and one drop are commonly felt around 60–80 BPM, often counted in half-time against double-time hats. Dub techno runs about 110–125 BPM. (The reggae range is a general convention I did not verify against primary sources; measure your references.)
- **Snare.** Tuned high and tight, with cross-stick/rimshot as the main backbeat voice. Timbales and syndrums appear as fills, especially at Channel One and on Shaka's sessions.

### D2. Harmony instruments
- **Skank / chop.** Guitar and/or piano on beats 2 and 4 (the off-beat in half-time feel), short and muted. Terrano describes it as the "piston of a steam engine." In dub it is muted in and out rather than played throughout.\[61\]
- **Organ bubble / shuffle.** A double-time organ figure, often alternating low and high chord fragments on the 16th-note off-beats, which "changes our perception of the rhythm."\[61\]
- **Melodica.** Pablo's Hohner Piano 26; minor-key, modal "Far East" melodies; heavy echo.\[16\]
- **Horns.** Trumpet, tenor and trombone in unison or octaves, with bluesy lines (Terrano: "keep the lead instruments and brass quite bluesy").\[61\] The Gibbs and Tubby-era sections included Tommy McCook, Bobby Ellis and Vin Gordon.\[76\]\[81\]
- **Bass.** Played high in the mix and laid back. Lines carry the hook, often leave beat 1 empty, and run from simple ostinatos to strongly syncopated patterns.\[61\] Note lengths are generally short and staccato against the drop. Register is mostly the low-E-to-A octave and below (my inference from listening; confirm with a spectrum analyser).
- **Keys.** Minor keys and modal vamps are common. Treat this as convention, not a verified statistic.

### D3. FM synthesis notes for the groovebox
These are design starting points from FM theory. They are not taken from the dub sources above.

| Voice | Suggested FM topology | Key parameters |
|---|---|---|
| **Dub bass** | 2-op: sine carrier, modulator ratio 1:1 (or 2:1 for a hollow tone); optional sub-sine carrier at ratio 0.5 | Index about 0.5–1.5 with fast decay (20–80 ms) for a "pluck," then near-pure sine sustain; amp decay 150–400 ms (staccato); LP after the operators about 600 Hz–1.2 kHz; mono, no detune |
| **Kick** | 1-op sine with pitch envelope (sweep about 150 Hz → 45–55 Hz in 30–60 ms), optional 1:1 modulator burst | Short index burst for the beater click |
| **Rimshot / cross-stick** | 2–3 ops at inharmonic ratios (e.g., 1:1.41, 1:3.5) plus a 3–8 ms noise burst; HP about 300 Hz | Amp decay 30–80 ms; high index (3–6) decaying fast; tune "pitch" to the riddim key |
| **Hi-hat / flyers** | 3–4 ops at high inharmonic ratios (e.g., ×1.0, ×1.48, ×2.27, ×3.6) feeding noise-like spectra; HP 6–8 kHz | Two envelopes: open (300–600 ms) and choke on the next step (implement as a choke group) |
| **Skank guitar / chop** | 2-op, ratio 1:3 or 1:1 with feedback for a bright, nasal attack | Index decays in about 30 ms; amp decay 60–120 ms; band-pass 400 Hz–3 kHz; send to spring by default |
| **Organ bubble** | Additive ratio stack (DX7 algorithm-32-style): 1, 2, 3, 4 (drawbar-like) + key-click op (high ratio, 5 ms) | Rotary or chorus post-FX; short gate length for the bubble |
| **Melodica** | 2-op with modulator self-feedback (saw/reed-like); carrier ratio 1, modulator 1 or 2 | Breath-like attack (20–60 ms), index tracks velocity or aftertouch; slow vibrato 4–5 Hz after delay; slight detune between two carriers for free-reed beating |
| **Brass** | Classic Chowning brass: 1:1, index envelope follows amplitude envelope (brighter as louder) | Attack 30–80 ms, small pitch scoop up; unison stack of three voices in octaves for "section" |
| **Siren** | 1 sine/tri op, pitch LFO (square or ramp), rate and depth on knobs; hold-to-sound gate | Feed it into the delay by default (the authentic use) |

**Firmware-level dub features (my recommendations):** per-voice mute with quantised "just before the beat" switching; per-voice send A (delay) and B (spring) with momentary "throw" buttons; a delay with in-loop HPF/LPF, saturation, a soft limiter and a self-send above unity; a stepped 10-position HPF macro on the master and on the returns; a spring model with an impulse "kick" trigger; a dual phaser with a reverse-sync LFO.

## Part F — Staged Mastery Path

**Stage 0 — Template (1 week).** Build the B5 routing in your DAW and on hardware. Map every mute, send and return self-send to physical controls (at least 16 encoders and 16 buttons). Include safety limiters in the loops.

**Stage 1 — Riddim literacy (2–3 weeks).** Program one drop, rockers, steppers and flyers at 70–80 BPM with your own FM voices: bass, rimshot, hats, chop, bubble. Daily drill: play along with the reference records and match the bass note lengths by ear.

**Stage 2 — Single-technique drills (3–4 weeks).** Spend one week each on C3 (stepped HPF), C4 (feedback and throws), C5 (spring shots), and C1 (mutes). Rules: one take, no undo, print every pass, and log timing errors against the grid.

**Stage 3 — Versioning sessions (2 weeks).** C2 deliverables from one multitrack, ideally a multitrack you tracked yourself with live drums. This puts Scientist's "tone and balance first" into practice. Then remix someone else's stems.

**Stage 4 — Style emulation (4–6 weeks).** Produce one dub each "in the manner of":
- **Tubby:** mono, stepped HPF, tape loop, struck spring, no phaser.
- **Perry:** four-track bounce limit, Bi-Phase on hats and chops, Grampian drum-bus parallel, found sounds.
- **ET:** clean balance, vocal snippets, comic found sounds.
- **Scientist:** pattern-switching, feedback explosions.
- **Sherwood:** reverb into phaser, a reversed pass, dry/wet tension.
- **Basic Channel:** chord-stab into a filtered feedback delay over 4/4 at 115–125 BPM.

Blind-test each against the references with a friend.

**Stage 5 — Live dub performance (ongoing).** Perform 20–30-minute sets from stems on a hardware desk or your groovebox. Mix in the conventions of Shaka-style sound system play: siren, a dubplate-length build, rewind culture. Record every set. Review against three metrics: (1) mute timing accuracy; (2) feedback control (no uncontrolled clipping, no dead air); (3) variety, per Scientist's "you never hear it the same way twice." The goal is a performance with the same riddim on three nights and three distinct structures.

## Caveats
- **Gear provenance is weak overall.** Most Tubby and Black Ark specs come from interviews decades later, liner notes, replica makers' marketing, and forums. The HPF step count, Tubby's echo device, the Black Ark desk model and spring brand, and the Blackwell 16-track story all have conflicting accounts, as noted above. The 9069B frequency list comes from emulation and replica vendors, not an Altec datasheet.
- **"Firsts" are contested.** The first dub, first dub album (Hudson, Chin/Randy's, Perry and others) and first digital riddim (Sleng Teng versus Red Rose's "Tempo") all have competing claims.\[11\]\[35\]
- **Scientist's accounts** are first-hand but self-interested (he disputes being "taught" by Tubby, and disputes others' credits).\[47\] Weigh them accordingly.
- **Gaps in this guide.** Exact track timestamps, tempo and key statistics, and the FM parameter values are my own engineering starting points or need checking against audio. They are not sourced facts. Veal's and Williams' academic treatments could not be consulted in full text for this guide and should be read directly for the filter and the history of Tubby's studio practice.
